SECTION 1 — THE MIRACLE AND THE DEAL
VO

To understand what that 1994 decision was — and why it matters so much — you first need to see what South Africa actually started with.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
split screen — 1994 election queue (left) / Mandela at the Union Buildings inauguration (right)
Custom motion graphic
VO

Most countries that get democracy for the first time start from near zero. South Africa was not most countries.

But here's the thing the celebrations missed.

There were actually two transitions happening in 1994. Not one.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
two-column graphic — "Political Transition" (tick) / "Economic Transition" (question mark)
Custom motion graphic
VO

The political transition was complete and extraordinary. One person, one vote. Free press. Independent courts. A new constitution, signed in 1996, that international legal scholars still hold up as one of the best ever written.

The economic transition? Didn't happen.

The mines were still owned by the same corporations as before. The large commercial farms — same families. The major companies on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange — same shareholders.

Now, was this an oversight? A failure of political will?

No. It was a deliberate decision. And the person who made it — Treasury Minister Trevor Manuel, with Mandela's full backing — was trying to prevent a catastrophe.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
newspaper front page graphic — headline: "Capital flight fears as ANC prepares for power. 1993."
Custom motion graphic
VO

In the years before the election, there had been enormous capital outflows from South Africa. Wealthy investors and corporations were pulling money out of the country, terrified of what a democratic government might do with the mines, the farms, the banks. The rand had collapsed. The fear was that if the ANC moved aggressively to redistribute economic assets on day one, the economy would collapse before the new democracy had any chance to prove itself.

So the ANC made a call.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
document graphic — "The RDP. Reconstruction and Development Programme. ANC. 1994."
Custom motion graphic
VO

They came in with a plan called the RDP — the Reconstruction and Development Programme. It was specific and ambitious. It proposed doubling the income share of South Africa's poorest forty percent within a decade. State-led redistribution. Direct investment in the majority.

This was the economic programme the liberation movement had fought for.

🔍
🔍 GENERAL
RDP document crossed out → replaced by "GEAR. Growth, Employment and Redistribution. 1996."
VO

Two years later, quietly and without a public referendum, it was replaced.

In 1996, under pressure from international financial institutions and South Africa's own corporate sector, the government switched to a new framework called GEAR. Where the RDP was redistributive and state-led, GEAR was its near opposite. Fiscal restraint. Privatisation. Market-led growth.

🔍
🔍 GENERAL
document cover — "Growth for All. South African Foundation. 1996."
VO

South Africa's fifty largest corporations had published a document that same year — literally called "Growth for All" — that outlined exactly this approach. The new government adopted it almost word for word.

Nelson Mandela, later, described GEAR as non-negotiable. A concession he felt he had to make to keep the country stable.

He may well have been right.

But here's what that concession actually meant in practice.

🔍

And this is where that leaves South Africa on the global inequality scale. Every country on earth, shaded from light to dark. The darker, the more unequal. Find South Africa.

It is the darkest shade on the map.

Not the most unequal in Africa. The most unequal country on earth.

And that line we looked at at the start of the video — the one that barely moved across thirty years of democracy? That is the direct consequence of the 1994 deal. Not of Zuma. Not of state capture.

Of a choice that was made with good intentions, under real pressure, by people who were trying to protect the new democracy.

That is what makes this story so hard. There's no villain here. There's just a decision. And its consequences.

SECTION 2 — THE STRUCTURAL TRAP
VO

So here's the question that naturally follows.

If the economic structure didn't change — if the mines, the farms, the JSE stayed in the same hands — where did economic opportunity actually come from for Black South Africans in the new democracy?

The answer to that question explains almost everything that happened next.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
two-column diagram — "Private Economy" (mostly closed) / "The State" (opened up under ANC control)
Custom motion graphic
VO

The private economy — the mines, the farms, the financial sector — stayed mostly closed to the majority. The 1994 deal locked that in.

But the state? The state was now run by the ANC. Government contracts. Public procurement budgets. Senior appointments at state-owned enterprises. That's where the opportunity was.

Think about what this means. If the only path to economic power runs through the government, then controlling the right part of the government stops being just about policy. It becomes about economic survival. For politicians and their networks, appointments to state institutions aren't just jobs — they are access to the only real wealth-creation mechanism available to them.

In that environment, one thing follows almost inevitably.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
org chart — government positions. Arrows from "ANC HQ" into each box. Labels read "LOYAL"
Custom motion graphic
VO

In 1997, the ANC formalised a policy called cadre deployment. Place loyal party members in senior positions across the state. The logic was reasonable at first — the old bureaucracy had been built under apartheid and couldn't be trusted. You needed people who actually believed in the new South Africa.

Fair enough.

🎨
🎨 CUSTOM
same org chart — experienced staff walking off screen. A revolving door appears.
Custom motion graphic
VO

But give that system twenty years without accountability and watch what happens. Positions stop going to the most capable person. They go to whoever is loyal to the right faction. When the faction changes — and they do — the people change too. All the institutional knowledge walks out the door.

This is not corruption in the simple sense of stealing money. It's more subtle than that. It's the systematic replacement of competence with political utility. And once it takes hold in an institution, it is extremely difficult to reverse — because the people who benefit from the system are also the people who would need to reform it.

📽

No institution makes this clearer than Eskom, South Africa's national power utility. In the 1990s, it was genuinely excellent — generating electricity so cheaply that it exported the surplus to neighbouring countries.

The government then decided to build two new power stations.

SECTION 3 — THE WEEK THE DATA BECAME REAL
VO

By July 2021, twenty-seven years of pressure had been building. The inequality from 1994 was unchanged. Youth unemployment was rising. The electricity was getting worse. And yes — a decade of serious looting under Zuma had drained real money from real institutions.

Then Jacob Zuma was sent to prison — not for corruption itself, as it turned out, but for refusing to cooperate with the corruption inquiry. Within hours, two provinces started burning.
