## [HOOK]

April 27th, 1994. South Africa.

[SHOW: archival footage — 1994 election queues at dawn]

The queues began before sunrise.

In Soweto. In the Eastern Cape. In the dusty townships of KwaZulu-Natal. Elderly women who had walked for hours. Men who had stood in line since the night before. People who had lived their entire lives inside a system that had told them, explicitly, in law, that they did not count — and who had come, today, to be counted.

One woman, eighty-two years old, told a journalist she had been waiting her whole life for this day. She had brought her registration card in both hands, as if it might disappear.

[SHOW: footage widens — thousands in queue]

By the time the last polls closed, twenty million South Africans had voted in the country's first fully democratic election. The queues had stretched for kilometres. People had waited twelve hours. Some had fainted in the heat and came back to wait again.

The world watched, and called it a miracle.

And honestly — it was.

That was thirty years ago. So what happened since?

If you have heard anything about South Africa lately, you have probably heard about corruption — a former president, Jacob Zuma, and a network of businessmen who used government contracts to steal on a massive scale. That really happened and some people have gone to prison for it.

## [SECTION 1 — 1994 TO 1996: FREEDOM AT THE BALLOT, NOT AT THE BANK]

[SHOW: timeline — 1994 highlighted]

To understand 1994, you need to know what came before it.

For decades, South Africa was run under apartheid — a legal system that separated people by race, denied Black South Africans the vote, restricted where they could live and work, and concentrated wealth in white hands. The African National Congress, or ANC, was the main party that fought to end that system. Its leader, Nelson Mandela, had spent twenty-seven years in prison for opposing apartheid.

In April 1994, apartheid ended at the ballot box. The ANC won the first free election. Mandela became president and, for the first time, everyone could vote.

[SHOW: two columns — "Politics changed" / "Who owned what — barely changed"]

But when the power and control over a country is transferred from one party to another, then there should really be two transitions happening at once — and only one of them reached completion.

The political transition, where every person was granted an equal vote, which was upheld by Independent courts and preserved by a free press. This was followed by a new constitution in 1996 that legal experts still describe as one of the strongest in the world.

And then there is the economic transition. Basically, what happens to the infrastructure? Who is now going to own the mines, the farms, the banks, the big companies on the stock exchange? How do you preserve value whilst correcting for past injustices?

[SHOW: land ownership bars — "7% of population → 73% of farmland"]

Look at this chart. White South Africans are only about seven in every hundred people in the country — but they still own roughly three quarters of private farmland. Thirty years into democracy, that share has barely moved.

This was one of the first of many issues plaguing the new government. In the early 1990s, money was leaving the country fast. Investors feared the ANC would seize mines and banks. These fears caused the currency to collapse. Mandela's government faced a brutal choice:

Option A: either break up white economic power and risk an immediate economic crisis, or

Option B: keep the old economic structure in place and try to grow the country out of poverty over time.

So they chose option B...

[SHOW: document — "RDP, 1994"]

They still had a plan to spread opportunity. It was called the RDP — the Reconstruction and Development Programme. The idea was to double the share of national income going to the poorest forty percent of South Africans within a decade, using the state to invest directly in housing, jobs, and services.

[SHOW: timeline — 1996. RDP crossed out → GEAR appears]

Two years later, they dropped it. Money was still leaving the country, the rand was under pressure, and the government swapped redistribution for budget cuts and privatization.

It was replaced by GEAR — Growth, Employment and Redistribution — which trusted growth and markets to reach the poorest over time, instead of the state spreading wealth directly. So by 1996, the trade-off was settled in plain terms. The ANC had political power. But the firms that already owned the mines, the banks, and most of the land kept their property, and got the economic rules they had pushed for.

Change economic policy in 1996, and most of the story since follows from that moment — the houses and grants on one side, the land, the jobs crisis, and the scandals on the other.

## [SECTION 2 — 1996 TO 2009: GROWTH ON PAPER, STAGNATION UNDERNEATH]

[SHOW: timeline — 1996–2007 highlighted. GDP line rising]

From 1996 to 2007, the economy grew about four percent a year. Millions of houses went up. Water and electricity reached communities that had never had them. On paper, Option B looked like it might work.

[SHOW: montage — houses, water pipes, classrooms]

But underneath the growth, the wealth at the top did not spread — because the people who owned the economy had not changed.

[SHOW: timeline — 1997 highlighted. Org chart — QUALIFIED → LOYAL]

In 1997, the ANC formalised cadre deployment — placing loyal party members in senior jobs across government and state-owned companies. At first that made sense: the old civil service had been built under apartheid.

The problem is what it became. The private economy was still largely closed to the majority, so the state became the main route to wealth — through contracts, procurement, and appointments at companies like Eskom. When loyalty matters as much as skill, institutions slowly stop working.

[SHOW: youth unemployment — 43% in 1994 → ~50% by 2007]

Youth unemployment was already climbing — from forty-three percent at democracy to around fifty percent by 2007. The deeper crisis was visible long before the president most people blame for corruption took office.

---

## [SECTION 3 — 2009 TO 2018: CORRUPTION GOT WORSE — BUT THE CRACKS WERE ALREADY THERE]

[SHOW: timeline — 2009 highlighted]

In 2009, Jacob Zuma became president. This is the period most international viewers know about — and the corruption in these years was real and enormous.

A wealthy business family, the Guptas, gained influence over cabinet appointments and state contracts. Billions of rand — South Africa's currency — were diverted from public institutions like Eskom and the national rail company into private pockets. A public inquiry called the Zondo Commission, which ran for three years from 2018, documented hundreds of people and thousands of pages of evidence.

[SHOW: timeline — full period. Inequality flat. Youth unemployment rising through Zuma era]

But if you zoom out on the timeline, corruption in this period made an existing crisis worse — it did not create the crisis from scratch.

Look back at those charts — none of that started under Zuma. Cadre deployment had already been weakening institutions for twelve years.

Think of it this way: Zuma was nine years of acceleration, not the beginning of the story.

[SHOW: timeline — 2015. Load shedding calendar]

By 2015, South Africa began experiencing load shedding — scheduled rolling blackouts because the power grid could not keep up with demand. The new power stations were years late, massively over budget, and badly built. Corruption played a role, but so did years of cadre deployment — putting politically connected managers in charge of a technical utility they did not understand.

[SHOW: timeline — 2018]

In 2018, the ANC forced Zuma out and Cyril Ramaphosa became president. The Zondo Commission began publishing its findings. Many people hoped that removing a corrupt president would turn the country around.

On corruption, some progress was made. On the deeper economic structure — not much changed at all.

---

## [SECTION 4 — 2018 TO TODAY: ACCOUNTABILITY, UNREST, AND A PARTIAL RECOVERY]

[SHOW: timeline — 2018–2024 highlighted]

Ramaphosa's government did prosecute some of the people involved in state capture, and South Africa's courts and free press largely held — which is significant, because many countries lose those institutions when scandals of this scale surface.

But prosecuting corruption is not the same as changing who owns the economy.

[SHOW: land reform — target 30% by 2014 / actual 11% by 2024]

The government once promised to transfer thirty percent of white-owned commercial farmland to Black owners by 2014. Thirty years on, the figure is about eleven percent. Direct Black ownership of major companies listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange is estimated at only three to ten percent.

[SHOW: timeline — July 2021. Map — KwaZulu-Natal and Gauteng]

In July 2021, Zuma was sent to prison for refusing to testify before the corruption inquiry. Within hours, two provinces erupted in the worst violence since apartheid ended. Over ten days, three hundred and fifty-four people were killed and damage ran to fifty billion rand.

Much of the international coverage framed this as a political riot by Zuma's supporters. Some of it was organised that way. But look at what people were actually taking from looted stores.

[SHOW: footage — food on floors, empty shelves]

Food. Baby formula. Cooking oil. Bread. Rice. Nappies.

Twenty-seven years after people stood in those election queues, some South Africans were looting warehouses for food.

[SHOW: quote card — SAHRC]

The government's own Human Rights Commission later found that the lives of many who took part still looked, in material terms, like the lives of people under apartheid.

[SHOW: timeline — May 2024. ANC vote share falling to 40%]

In May 2024, the ANC received only forty percent of the vote — the first time in thirty years it could not govern alone. It formed a coalition government with rival parties. Within a year, something real improved: Eskom got new management, and rolling blackouts largely stopped. By late 2025, the country went months without losing power.

[SHOW: split card — electricity fixed / land, jobs, ownership unchanged]

That being said, the structural problems around who owns the land, who owns the economy, whether young people can find work. Yeah, those have not moved.

[SHOW: unemployment dials — 33% official / 61% ages 15–24. Icon grid — 6 in 10 greyed]

Today, one in three South Africans who want work cannot find it. For young people, it is six in ten — and that has been building across four different presidents, not one.

[SHOW: Sandton–Alexandra aerial — "500 metres apart"]

And this image still defines the country: Sandton, one of Africa's richest financial districts, and Alexandra, a densely packed township with poor services, sitting five hundred metres apart in the same city, thirty years into democracy.

## [SECTION 5 — WHAT ACTUALLY WENT WRONG, AND WHAT HAS TO CHANGE]

[SHOW: timeline — full 1994–2024. Three layers labelled]

Let me bring the thirty years together in plain terms.

What went wrong happened in three layers, and most news coverage only talks about the top one.

The bottom layer is the decision made in the mid-1990s: political freedom without changing who owned the economy. The redistribution plan was dropped.

The middle layer is what that produced — a state run for loyalty instead of competence, a crumbling power grid, and institutions that stopped working the way they should.

The top layer is the Zuma-era looting — real, criminal, and documented. It made everything worse, faster, but it was not where the crisis began.

[SHOW: title card — "Political freedom ≠ Economic freedom"]

South Africa has already tried the corruption fix — Zuma out in 2018, a new coalition in 2024, the lights back on in 2025.

Cleaner government helps. The real work is finishing the economic transition that was postponed in 1996.

I also want to be straight about why this is still so difficult in 2026 — none of this is a switch a president flips in one term.

	🎥  SELF

The 1996 bargain is still baked in. Patronage took decades to build. No single party runs the country alone anymore. That is a brutal hand — but it is why fixing Eskom was never going to finish the job.

[SHOW: data card — "What would actually move this" — 3 bullets: land measured publicly / open procurement / competent state appointments]
	🎨  CUSTOM

[SHOW: Abdullah on camera]
	🎥  SELF

Three things that would actually move this: land that changes hands and gets measured publicly. Procurement that is open before contracts are signed. And state companies that hire for competence, not loyalty. Those are not radical demands. They are the unfinished work of the 1994 promise.

[SHOW: 1994 queue footage returns]

The people in those 1994 queues were not voting only for the right to vote. They were voting for a country where their children could have what they never did — jobs, land, dignity.

They won the first revolution at the ballot box — a constitution, independent courts, and the right to stand in line after a lifetime of being told they did not count, and finally to be counted.

The second revolution is still economic: who owns the land, who owns the economy, and whether a young person can build a life without leaving their dignity at the gate. That fight was postponed in 1996, not lost, and what was postponed can still be finished.

[SHOW: Mandela inauguration]

On his inauguration day, Nelson Mandela said:

[SHOW: quote — white on black, no music]

*"Never, never, and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another."*

[PAUSE]

He was burying apartheid, but he was also drawing a line in the sand for every government that came after: freedom has to show up in ordinary life — in the wage, the roof over your head, the future your child is allowed to imagine.

[SHOW: Sandton–Alexandra aerial — hold 8 seconds]

This country has already done the impossible once — it broke apartheid without civil war, built institutions that still stand when scandals hit, and went back to the polls in 2024 when the old party finally bled support. South Africans have not run out of fight yet.

When you look at this image, you are seeing one of Africa's richest districts and one of its poorest townships in the same city, five hundred metres apart, thirty years after everyone voted.

After thirty years, a gap like that is still a policy choice — and it will only stay there if the people in power keep treating the rest of the promise as optional.

The hope from 1994 is still alive in every house built, every grant that keeps a family alive tonight, and in every young person who still refuses to accept a broken normal and call it fate.

South Africa has had enough miracle headlines. The country still needs political freedom and economic freedom in the same place, five hundred metres apart, with the rest of the promise actually delivered.

What began in 1994 was never meant to end at the ballot box alone, and that race is still running — for South Africa, and for any country that has raised a flag without changing who owns the ground beneath it.
