## The Core Identity

Thomas Mulligan is the **thought experiment channel**. Unlike Cleo Abram (science explanation) or Doug Sharpe (fact delivery), Mulligan's primary mode is the **second-person survival scenario**: "What would you do if..." or "What happens when..."

His channel has the tightest performance range of all six creators analyzed — all six videos fall within a 1.36x band (22.5M to 30.5M). This extreme consistency suggests the format is so well-calibrated that content quality matters less than format execution.

## Script Structure

### The Two Core Formats

**Format 1: The Cascading Consequence Scenario**
```
"[Setup: Let's say you wake up / What would happen if...]"
[Timeline of consequences at increasing intervals]
[Specific actionable survival advice or surprising fact]
[Final consequence — the one that reframes everything]
```

**Format 2: The Counterintuitive Fact**
```
[Setup — "Please don't do this" or "[Title] might be the scariest experiment"]
[Expert opinion vs. popular belief — the conflict]
[Resolution — what the science actually says]
[The uncomfortable implication]
```

---

### Example: "The World Without Humans" (30.5M)
> *"You know what would happen if all humans suddenly disappeared from the planet?"*  
> *"After about 1 week all of the lights would turn off..."*  
> *"After about 2 weeks all of the house pets... would pass away..."*  
> *"After 1 month all the nuclear power plants would become unstable..."*  
> *"After about 1 year any City not affected by nuclear fallout would be completely reclaimed by Mother Nature..."*  
> *"After 200 years all of our largest buildings and structures would begin to collapse..."*  
> *"Within 35,000 years the plant cycle removes all traces of lead..."*  
> *"65,000 years after that CO2 levels... return to pre-human levels..."*  
> *"Mount Rushmore survives for 7.2 million years..."*  
> *"The last traces of humanity will be our Bronze Statues and cities buried underground like some sort of tech fossil."*

This is a **timeline compression** technique — the viewer is pulled through millennia in under a minute, with each time marker more shocking than the last. The ending reframes all human achievement as geological artifact.

### Example: "The Last Person on Earth" (23.5M)
The same scenario unpacked from the survivor's perspective — this time second-person ("you"), making it a survival guide:
- 1 week: power grids go dark
- 2 weeks: free trapped pets
- Eat imported foods before they expire
- Find nearest nuclear reactor on a map
- 2 years to get to your permanent location before fuel expires

The second-person framing transforms a thought experiment into a **checklist** — people feel the urgency of actually planning, which drives comments ("I would go to...").

### Example: "Universe 25" (22.5M)
The scariest psychological experiment repackaged as a 58-second science short:
> *"Universe 25 might be the scariest experiment of all time..."*  
> [Setup: mice utopia with infinite resources]  
> [Descent: overcrowding, social breakdown, violence, extinction]  
> *"In an enclosure with enough space and enough food for 5,000."*

The final line lands as a philosophical gut-punch: the mice had everything and still died. No commentary needed.

## What Works

### 1. The "Last Person on Earth" and "World Without Humans" Are the Same Video Twice — Both Hit 30M
This is the most important data point in this analysis. Mulligan ran the same basic concept twice — what happens when humans disappear — and both videos reached 25–30M views. The first focuses on the planet's recovery ("The World Without Humans"), the second on the survivor's experience ("The Last Person on Earth").

**Lesson:** A strong concept can be mined from multiple angles. Don't exhaust a premise in one video — re-enter it from a different perspective.

### 2. The Cascading Timeline Is Perfectly Designed for Shorts
The format *weeks → months → years → thousands of years* creates a natural rhythm that matches the Shorts scroll behavior. Each unit of time is a new fact. Each fact is a mini-hook that prevents the viewer from swiping. The pacing is designed to have the viewer thinking "wait, what happens at 200 years?" before "1 year" is even finished.

### 3. Second-Person Creates Immersion Without Production Budget
"You have about 1 week left of electricity" is more engaging than "Humans would lose electricity within 1 week" even though they contain identical information. The second-person framing makes the viewer the protagonist of the thought experiment, which activates imagination rather than passive reception.

### 4. The "Please Don't Do This" Opener Outperforms Both Other Formats
"Can You Drink Glacier Water?" opens with *"Please don't ever do this."* — a direct address that functions as a warning, a tease, and a mystery simultaneously. The viewer needs to know: what are you doing, and why is it dangerous? This is arguably the best single opener in the entire analysis — it creates maximum intrigue with minimum setup.

### 5. "The Greatest Shot in Television History" Is an Outlier — and Still Works
A 57-second video about a specific moment in television history achieves 29M views despite having no hypothetical, no science, and no second-person framing. This suggests Mulligan's audience follows the *creator* more than the *format* — a sign of genuine parasocial connection above-average for this tier of channel.

### 6. Universe 25: The Named Experiment Formula
Naming the experiment ("Universe 25") rather than describing it ("what happens to mice with unlimited resources") adds credibility and mystery. The viewer feels they're being let in on something with a proper name — it sounds like something they should have learned in school.

## What Doesn't Work

### 1. The Ceiling Is Very Defined
The 22.5M–30.5M range is incredibly tight — no video breaks out significantly beyond 30M. The format appears to have a hard performance ceiling, possibly because the hypothetical/thought experiment format drives high initial plays but lower share rates than formats that generate specific debate or disagreement.

### 2. No Companion Video Strategy
Unlike Cleo Abram (who teases "we're making a longer video" and builds franchise curiosity), Mulligan's videos are self-contained. Each one ends without pulling the viewer toward the next. The result: consistent floor but no breakout potential.

### 3. Capitalized Single-Word Titles Underperform
"SPACE PHOTOGRAPHY" (25.8M) is the lowest performer and has the least descriptive title. Without a question, a hypothesis, or a "scariest/greatest" superlative, the title doesn't generate the specific curiosity gap that the other videos create. All-caps doesn't compensate for unclear subject matter.

## The Psychological Engine

Every Thomas Mulligan video exploits one of two triggers:

**Trigger 1: Self-preservation instinct.** "Last Person on Earth" and "World Without Humans" activate the viewer's survival planning instinct. People imagine themselves in the scenario and feel a mild urgency to think through what they'd actually do. This generates comments like "I would go to [specific location] because..." — highly personal and thus high-comment-count videos.

**Trigger 2: Violated expectations about the familiar.** "Can You Drink Glacier Water?" starts with "please don't ever do this" — implying something the viewer thought was safe is actually dangerous. "Universe 25" implies that abundance (something we assume is positive) leads to collapse. Both violate assumptions about safe/dangerous and good/bad, which is profoundly engaging.

## Key Takeaways for Scripting

1. **"Let's say you wake up tomorrow..." is the most powerful hypothetical opener.** It's second-person, immediate, and establishes the scenario in 6 words.
2. **Use cascading time intervals.** "After 1 week... after 1 month... after 200 years..." Each interval should be a new and surprising consequence.
3. **The "please don't do this" opener should be in your toolkit.** It establishes stakes and mystery before the viewer even knows the topic.
4. **Mine the same concept twice from different angles.** Planet without humans + survivor's perspective = two 30M videos.
5. **Name the experiment or phenomenon.** "Universe 25" sounds like something you should know. "An experiment with mice" does not.
6. **Run 50–59 seconds.** Long enough for a full consequence cascade, short enough for near-100% completion.
7. **The second-person "you" transforms information into participation.** The viewer isn't learning — they're surviving.
