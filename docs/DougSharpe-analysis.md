## The Core Identity

Doug Sharpe is the most **algorithmically consistent** channel in this analysis. With 1.36M subscribers averaging 25.5M views per video, the views-to-subscriber ratio is ~18x — meaning the algorithm distributes every video aggressively to non-subscribers.

The formula is locked, immovable, and never varies. That is the entire strategy.

## The Invariant Formula

Doug Sharpe has discovered something very few creators acknowledge: **a completely locked format can be a competitive moat.** When every single video opens with "Fun fact," uses the same pacing, ends with the same ironic kicker, and runs under 60 seconds — the viewer's brain knows exactly what to expect. That predictability is not boring; it's comfortable. It creates a Pavlovian response to the phrase "Fun fact."

### Script Structure
```
"Fun fact," [immediate story, no wind-up]
[Event described with specific names, dates, and concrete details]
[What should have happened / what everyone expected]
[What actually happened instead — the absurd or ironic twist]
[Ironic/deadpan final quote or kicker — usually from the subject of the story]
```

### Example (Canadian Man Fills Potholes):
> *"Fun fact, a Canadian man named John McHugh became so frustrated with potholes in Nova Scotia he began filling them himself..."*  
> [Story unfolds]  
> *"When asked what he would do with the money drivers had given him, he said he was starting a new job out west and would use the money to cover travel and living expenses. He then changed his mind and said, 'Not gonna lie, I'm probably gonna buy weed with it.'"*

### Example (The Most Canadian Crime Ever):
> *"Fun fact, his motivations were unclear, but on May 26th, a man kicked in the door at the Sweet Something Bakery in Vancouver..."*  
> [Story unfolds — burglar feels bad, cleans up, takes selfies, steals 6 cupcakes]  
> *"The store owner... created a signature cupcake in honor of the man called the Crime of Passion Cupcake."*

Both kickers work the same way: the ending is **more absurd than you expected**, and it comes from the subject of the story themselves. Doug doesn't editorialize — the story editorializes itself.

## What Works

### 1. "Fun fact," Is the Most Powerful Two Words on YouTube Shorts
The phrase "Fun fact" does three things simultaneously:
- **Signals brevity** — this is one fact, not a lecture
- **Signals credibility** — "fact" implies research
- **Creates a promise** — the viewer knows they'll learn something interesting in under a minute

Because Doug uses it invariantly, the phrase has become his personal brand signal. Within two words, returning viewers know exactly what format they're in.

### 2. Specific Names, Places, and Numbers — Always
Every fact story contains at least one hyper-specific detail that makes the story feel verified, not paraphrased:
- *"John McHugh"* (not "a man")
- *"Sweet Something Bakery in Vancouver"* (not "a bakery")
- *"six chocolate champagne cupcakes"* (not "some cupcakes")
- *"19,869,669 views"* on The Most Canadian Crime Ever

This specificity creates credibility without sourcing. The viewer doesn't need a citation because the details feel too precise to be invented.

### 3. Canadian Content Punches Above Its Weight
Three of the top four videos are Canadian stories (potholes, crime, Volvo car concept was Volvo but Canadian-themed on the channel). The Most Canadian Crime (19.8M) and Canadian Man Fills Potholes (32.3M) both achieve massive view counts despite being geographically specific stories.

**Why:** Canadian stories carry a built-in self-deprecating charm that resonates globally. The inherent niceness of Canadian mishaps makes them feel warm, not threatening. Non-Canadians enjoy the stereotype being confirmed; Canadians enjoy the affectionate ribbing.

### 4. The 40–60 Second Sweet Spot
The highest-performing videos cluster around 47-59 seconds. The two shortest (29s) still perform strongly (20-24M), but the most engaged videos (35K+ comments) run 47-59 seconds. This suggests there's enough time for the story to breathe — to include the setup, the unexpected twist, and the final kicker — without testing patience.

### 5. No Opinion, No Editorializing
Doug never says "isn't that crazy?" or "I couldn't believe this." The story does the work. This removes the creator's ego from the frame and lets the viewer form their own reaction — which they then express in the comments.

The 35,000 comments on the Volvo car video and 14,000 on The Most Canadian Crime are pure audience reaction because Doug never told them how to feel.

### 6. The Ironic Kicker as Closing
The final line of every story is the most ironic, absurd, or self-revealing thing the subject said or did. It functions as a "button" — a theatrical term for the comic button that punctuates a scene. Doug has found a perfect repeating button format:
- The subject reveals their true motive (usually delightfully petty or absurdly reasonable)
- The community/institution's response to chaos is disproportionately generous (Crime of Passion Cupcake)
- The original "logical" purpose of something is revealed as hilariously impractical (Volvo — no hood because nobody uses it except to refill washer fluid)

## What Doesn't Work

### 1. Corporate/Tech Stories Underperform vs. Human Stories
Alexa Is Always Listening (17.2M) and Kansas Is Flatter Than A Pancake (20.8M) perform at the lower end of the range. Both are fact-based but lack a **human protagonist doing something absurd**. The channel's strongest videos all have a named person making a memorable decision. Without that human element, the fact is merely interesting — not shareable.

### 2. The Format Has No Floor
While consistency is the channel's moat, it also means there's no format innovation possible. Every video is, by definition, the same shape. This caps the channel's ability to reach new audiences through novelty or to generate "event" content that gets press coverage or cross-promotion. It also means the channel's future is entirely dependent on finding increasingly good source stories — a content sourcing problem, not a production problem.

### 3. The #funfact Hashtag May Be Limiting Discovery
All titles end with `#funfact`. While this builds brand consistency, it also signals to the algorithm that the content belongs to a specific hashtag bucket — potentially limiting cross-recommendation to viewers who aren't already in the "fact short" audience.

## Title Anatomy

Every title follows: **[The Story's Punchline or Most Surprising Element] #funfact**

The title IS the fact — Doug's titles function as one-line summaries that promise the whole story:
- "A Car Designed By Women For Women" → "I need to know more about this"
- "Canadian Man Fills Potholes" → "Wait, that's a story?"
- "2 Year Old Boy Orders 31 Cheese Burgers from Door Dash" → "How is that even possible?"

Each title creates a **specific curiosity gap** — not "this is strange" but "I specifically need the explanation for THIS particular thing."

## Key Takeaways for Scripting

1. **"Fun fact," is non-negotiable. Every. Single. Time.** The invariance IS the brand.
2. **Open with the name of the person.** Never "a man." Always "John McHugh."
3. **Give the location.** Not "a bakery." "The Sweet Something Bakery in Vancouver."
4. **No commentary. No "isn't that wild?"** If you say it, you've stolen the reaction from your audience.
5. **The final line must be the most absurd thing the subject said or did.** It should be a direct quote if possible.
6. **Run 45–60 seconds.** Long enough for a full story arc. Short enough that the completion rate stays near 100%.
7. **Source real, niche, specific stories.** The strength is in the sourcing — small-market local news stories that went locally viral but haven't reached the global audience yet.
