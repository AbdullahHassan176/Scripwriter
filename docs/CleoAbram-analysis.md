## The Core Identity

Cleo Abram is a **curiosity curator** operating in the "science made human" space. Her short-form videos occupy a precise psychological niche: **the question you've always wanted answered but never thought to Google.**

Every video starts from a premise the viewer already half-knows ("digging a hole through the earth") and delivers the actual physics answer in a way that makes them feel smart for asking. The subject matter is always something the viewer could explain to a friend at dinner — which is the primary sharing trigger for science content.

The channel's exceptional subscriber-to-view ratio (6.8x) indicates strong algorithmic distribution, driven by high completion rates and share rates on genuinely surprising answers to familiar questions.

## Script Structure

### The Three-Part Short Formula

```
[Question or deceptively simple premise]
[Layered answer — starts familiar, adds unexpected detail]
[The fact that blows the mind — usually a comparison to something known]
[Optional: tease to longer content or follow]
```

### Example: "What If You Dig A Hole Through The Earth?"
> *"What would happen if you dug a hole straight through the Earth and then jumped in? Would you get stuck in the middle? Would you bounce back and forth forever?"*  
> [Physics explanation unfolds — gravity, acceleration, 11 km/s at center]  
> *"And so you're bouncing back and forth basically forever."*  
> *"In real life the deepest anyone has ever dug is a third of the way through the crust in the 1970s and we're making a longer video all about it so subscribe..."*

### Example: "How Deep The Ocean REALLY Is"
> *"Most people don't understand how deep the ocean really is."*  
> [Sequential depth scale — 10m, 214m, 830m, 1,000m, 1,220m, 3,800m (Titanic), 10,984m]  
> *"If you put Mount Everest into the water upside down there would still be over a mile until you got to the bottom."*  
> *"The ocean is just amazing but the thing is we haven't mapped it very well..."*

The "How Deep The Ocean" structure is particularly instructive: it's a **walking scale** — each layer is larger than the last, and each reference point is something the viewer already knows (diver, skyscraper, submarine, Titanic). The viewer learns by comparison, not by memorizing abstract numbers.

## What Works

### 1. The "Digging" Franchise
"What If You Dig A Hole Through The Earth?" (93.6M) and "What If You Just Keep Digging?" (51.9M) are thematically paired and together account for ~145M views. This is the most important structural finding in this analysis:

**A question that generates a question is more valuable than a question that generates an answer.**

"What If You Dig A Hole?" naturally generates "But how deep has anyone actually dug?" — which is exactly the next video. Cleo is building a **curiosity franchise**, not just individual shorts. The viewer who watches one digging video has already been primed for the next one.

### 2. The Familiar → Mind-Blowing Arc
Every video starts with something the viewer thinks they already know, then reveals that their mental model is completely wrong. The gap between expectation and reality is the engagement engine:

- "You know the Earth is big" → you would **bounce back and forth forever**
- "The ocean is deep" → if you put **Mount Everest in upside down**, there's still over a mile to the bottom
- "Captchas use images" → robots **literally cannot perceive randomness** the way humans do

The viewer feels surprised and then smart for understanding the surprise. That combination is shareable.

### 3. The Depth Scale Technique
The ocean video demonstrates a powerful teaching technique for incomprehensible scales: **anchor each level to something the viewer knows.**

- 214m = deepest unaided human dive (personal reference)
- 830m = tallest building on Earth (visual reference)
- 3,800m = Titanic (cultural reference)
- 10,984m = Challenger Deep (the unknown, now contextualized)

This technique makes abstract numbers visceral. It's not "10,984 meters" — it's "Mount Everest upside down and still more than a mile to go."

### 4. "Follow for More" vs. "Subscribe"
Cleo ends most videos with "follow for more" (the TikTok/Shorts native CTA) rather than "subscribe." This is intentional: "follow" has lower psychological friction than "subscribe" and fits the casual, conversational tone of the content. The CTA never feels like a business ask; it feels like a recommendation between friends.

### 5. No Editorializing on Wonder
Cleo never tells you to be amazed. She presents the fact and trusts the viewer to generate their own wonder:
- *"The ocean is just amazing"* — one sentence, after minutes of layered revelation
- *"It's crazy though that the deepest hole humanity has ever dug is still 0.002% of the way to the middle of the Earth"*

This minimal commentary is paired with maximum factual payoff. The viewer isn't told to feel; they just do.

### 6. Comments as Curiosity Spillover
The "What If You Dig" video has 27,000 comments — the most in this analysis. Science content with genuinely surprising answers generates comments like "But what about the pressure??" and "What happens at the center?" — questions the viewer didn't think to ask before watching. This comment activity signals to the algorithm that the video is intellectually engaging, not just passively viewed.

## What Doesn't Work

### 1. The Animal Guessing Format Is a Different Show
"Can You Guess This Animal?" (38.2M) is the weakest performer relative to the others. It's essentially a visual puzzle — a different content mode than science explanation. Despite 38M views, it generates only 6,600 comments vs. 27,000 for the digging video. The format doesn't generate the curiosity-cascade that the physics videos do, which means it's less likely to drive subscriptions and watch-through behavior.

### 2. The "Longer Video" Tease Needs an On-Screen Link
*"We're making a longer video all about it so subscribe"* — this is a strong CTA for a paired companion video, but only if the algorithm serves the longer video to the same viewer immediately after. Without an explicit linked card or pinned comment, viewers who want more have to go find it, which most won't.

### 3. 35-Second Videos Leave Potential on the Table
The 35-second "Underwater Tunnels" (71.6M) and 36-second "Guess This Animal" (38.2M) are the shortest in the set. The best-performing videos are 48-59 seconds. This suggests the middle-of-the-range depth (2-3 extra comparative facts) is where the real engagement lives. The shortest videos feel like they leave the topic before the viewer is fully satisfied.

## Title Formulas That Perform

**Tier 1 (50M+ views):**
- "What If You [Impossible/Extreme Physical Action]?" → 93M, 51M
- "How [X] REALLY Is" (the "REALLY" modifier signals hidden depth) → 33M

**Tier 2 (30–50M):**
- "Why Can't [Technology] Do [Obviously Simple Human Task]?" → 34M
- "How Do They [Impossible Engineering Feat]?" → 71M

**Works because:** All titles share the "I know something about this but not the full answer" structure. The viewer is 50% curious before they click.

## Key Takeaways for Scripting

1. **Never start with the answer.** Start with the question the viewer already has but never asked.
2. **Use the depth scale technique.** Abstract numbers mean nothing. "The Titanic" means everything.
3. **Build franchises, not just videos.** One video should naturally generate the question that the next video answers.
4. **"The ocean is just amazing."** One sentence of wonder at the end, after all the facts. Don't editorialize during; only after.
5. **Target 48–58 seconds.** Long enough to layer 3-4 facts; short enough to hit near-100% completion.
6. **Your title should be a question the viewer already half-knows the answer to.** The tension is in the gap between their guess and the truth.
