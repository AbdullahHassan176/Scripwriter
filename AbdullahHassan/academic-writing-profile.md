## 1. Two Voices From the Same Mind

You do **not** write the same way you **present**. Both are analytical, but the channel changes the rules.

| Dimension | Written academic (essays, papers) | Spoken academic (seminars, oral exams) |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Formality** | High — journal/actuarial convention | Medium — classroom-professional |
| **Sentence length** | Median ~17 words; frequent 25–45 word analytical sentences | Median ~18 words; more questions and one-line pivots |
| **Person** | Impersonal: “this paper”, “the literature”, “it is proposed” | First-person plural: “we”, “our paper”, “we want you to…” |
| **Humour** | Essentially none | Dry, occasional, audience-aware |
| **Structure** | Numbered sections (`1.1.1`) | Framework-first (e.g. Actuarial Control Cycle) + slides |
| **Evidence** | Citations every claim; tables | Polls, quotes, analogies, “follow the sound of my voice” |

When an AI drafts **for you**, ask which register. Default for PDFs/submissions: **written academic**. Default for scripts you will read aloud: **spoken academic** (seminar row).

## 2. Sentence Length & Rhythm

Automated pass over your PDF prose (493 sentences, practice essay + research draft):

| Metric | Your academic PDFs |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Mean length** | ~22 words |
| **Median** | ~17 words |
| **75th percentile** | ~31 words |
| **90th percentile** | ~46 words |
| **Short (≤12 words)** | ~33% of sentences |
| **Long (≥35 words)** | ~18% of sentences |

**What this means in practice:**

- You are **not** a staccato writer in academic mode. You use **medium-long compound sentences** to carry policy logic, then **shorter sentences** to land definitions or turns.
- A typical paragraph **opens** with context or definition (often medium length), **develops** with a long sentence listing mechanisms, reforms, or data, then **turns** with “However,” “Unfortunately,” or “Despite…” (often shorter or mid-length).
- You tolerate — and often prefer — **one sentence per numbered sub-point** (`1.1.1`) when stating a precise claim, followed by elaboration in the next sentence.

**Seminar script (333 sentences):** Slightly shorter on average (~21 words mean), more **fragment-adjacent** lines for slides (“financial perspective”, “Popularity”) — those are delivery cues, not essay style.

### Rhythm you like

```
[Definition — medium]
Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is the obtainment of a comprehensive range of health services
with the certainty of financial risk protection.

[Context + contrast — long, "however" embedded]
Since the introduction of the national health insurance initiatives in the post-apartheid period,
South Africa has technically complied with the basic requirements of UHC, however various
inadequacies have existed within the policies aimed at pooling and resource allocation.

[Explicit purpose — medium, quoted question]
The purpose of this paper is to answer the following: "What are the policy inefficiencies…?"
```

### Rhythm you dislike (in written work)

- Chains of five-plus **fragments** without verbs (YouTube rule applies here too when drafting essays).
- **Uniform** sentence length — every sentence 20–25 words feels mechanical.
- **One-sentence paragraphs** repeated for drama (you use dense paragraphs with internal structure instead).

## 3. Document Structure (What You Always Build)

### Front matter (long papers)

1. Title (often dual-line: topic + subtitle with scope)
2. **ABSTRACT** — scope, method, countries, explicit mention of **limitations**
3. **KEYWORDS**
4. **CONTACT DETAILS** (full name, school, student number, phone, email)
5. By-line: `By A Hassan` or `By A Hassan & [co-author]`

### Body architecture (research draft pattern)

```
1. INTRODUCTION
   1.1 Topic importance (definitions + global/South African policy hooks)
   1.2 National context
   1.3 Specific reform (e.g. NHI)
   1.4 Contemporary shock (COVID-19) — tied to system preparedness
   1.5 Plan of development (roadmap paragraph)

2. AIM
   2.1 Single aim statement ("holistic perspective" is signature phrasing)
   2.2 Research questions as − bullet list
   2.3 Contribution to literature

3. METHODOLOGY OF ANALYSIS
   (literature review + comparative framework + metrics)

4+. THEMATIC SECTIONS (policy, country models, SA application)

DISCUSSION / CONCLUSIONS
REFERENCES (long, varied sources — WHO, Lancet, NDoH, World Bank)
```

### Shorter essays (practice essay pattern)

```
Title
By A Hassan
CONTACT DETAILS
1. INTRODUCTION (numbered 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 — purpose ends with quoted RQ)
2. LITERATURE REVIEW (numbered findings 1) 2) 3))
3. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS (preliminary answer + future work)
REFERENCES
```

### Lists

- **Research questions / challenges:** leading hyphen `−` (not always ASCII `-`)
- **Enumerated findings in lit review:** `1)` `2)` `3)` then closing synthesis paragraph
- **Policy channels / components:** `−` bullet blocks under a lead-in sentence
- **Tables:** Country × indicator grids; separate **indicator definition** subsections (e.g. `3.7 Explanation of Indicators`)

You **like** the reader always knowing **where they are** (`1.4.3`, `¶3.5`, “section 4 below”, “Plan of Development”).

You **dislike** unstructured “essay-shaped” prose with no numbered spine — your seminar team literally calls a paper **“amorphous”** when authors do that.

## 4. How You Argue (Logic Moves You Repeat)

### The “agree — then extend” move

You rarely attack literature outright. You **credit**, then **add**:

- *“The approaches taken in the literature are appropriate **yet** there are other…”*
- *“The preliminary answer… **agrees upon** the points suggested by the literature **and suggests**…”*
- *“Unfortunately, despite the commitment to…”*

This is your default dialectic. AI should not write pure contrarian essays without first stating what existing work gets right.

### Layered causation (actuarial / systems instinct)

You separate:

- **Design** (financing, pooling, purchasing, benefit structures)
- **Implementation** (governance, leadership, cadre-style institutional issues in other work)
- **Shocks** (COVID-19 as stress test, corruption as exacerbator — not root cause in SA economic video work; in COVID draft, corruption **exacerbates** crisis response)

You **hate** single-variable explanations (“NHI failed because of X only”) without showing the stack.

### Scope discipline

You **like**:

- “**preliminary** answer”
- “**allowing for notable exceptions**” (country selection)
- “explicit account taken of the **limitations** of the health indicators”
- “**Further investigations**… are required”
- “**Whilst** investigating, care will be taken…”

You **dislike**:

- Overclaiming (“proves”, “definitively shows”) without data
- Hiding metric weaknesses when comparing countries

### Comparative method

- Basket of countries: **LMIC + HIC**, Brazil, Chile, Ghana, Thailand, Germany, UK, South Africa, etc.
- Same indicators across countries → interpret in prose
- SA section always **feeds back** to NHI / policy design

## 5. How You Start Sentences (Likes vs Dislikes)

### Written academic — openings you use often (keep these)

| Opener type | Examples from your work | Function |
|-------------|-------------------------|----------|
| **The + noun** | “The literature identifies…”, “The purpose of this paper…” | Neutral authority |
| **This + noun** | “This paper takes…”, “This literature review provides…” | Frame your contribution |
| **Given / In aiming / To achieve** | “Given the limited resources…”, “In aiming to achieve UHC…” | Policy context |
| **However / Unfortunately / Despite** | “However, Ghana has among the lowest…”, “Unfortunately, despite the commitment…” | Turn / problematise |
| **It is + proposed/believed/important** | “It is proposed that the NHI Fund…”, “It is believed that this objective…” | Formal passive framing |
| **According to** | “According to the World Health Report…” | Cite anchor |
| **Numbered lead-in** | “1.1.1 Achieving Universal Health Coverage…” | Section contract |
| **South Africa / The country** | “South Africa reported its first COVID-19 case…” | National anchor |

**Transition density (your PDFs):** `However` (~13), `Despite` (~11), `It is` (~11), `According to` (~7), `Thus` (~7), `as well as` (~16), `whilst` (~5), `holistic` (~5).

### Written academic — openings you avoid or barely use (don’t start like this)

| Avoid | Why |
|-------|-----|
| **“In this essay I will…”** | Throat-clearing; you state purpose via *“The purpose of this paper is to answer…”* |
| **“So,” / “Well,” / “Okay,”** | Seminar-only; almost absent in PDFs |
| **“I think” / “I believe”** (unqualified) | You use *“it is believed”* or cite someone |
| **“Basically,” / “Literally,”** | Informal; not in your academic corpus |
| **“Everyone knows…”** | You define terms instead |
| **“In today’s world…”** | Generic filler |
| **Rhetorical question** (as first sentence of section) | Rare in PDFs; common in seminar |
| **“In conclusion,”** (as section opener) | You use **DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS** as heading, not throat-clearing phrase (only 1× “in conclusion” in corpus) |

### Spoken academic — how you actually start lines (seminar)

You **do** open with:

- *“Good morning everyone.”*
- *“Okay. So for your viewing and auditory experience…”*
- *“At the end of this seminar, we want you to be able to answer…”*
- *“Let’s start from the beginning. Imagine you find yourself…”*
- *“But why does performance measurement play…”*
- *“Thus, the basis of this paper is…”*
- *“This begs the question: what is stakeholder theory…”*
- *“As statistics graduates, I’m sure this originally sounds quite problematic…”*

You **hand off** with: *“Abdullah will now…”*, *“Back to you, Abdullah”*, *“Dylan will now take you through…”*

## 6. How You End Sentences & Paragraphs (Likes vs Dislikes)

### Endings you like

- **Citation close:** `...governance and transparent accountability mechanisms (Martin et al., 2014).`
- **Forward point:** `...will be further expanded on in section 4 below.`
- **Future work:** `...are required to further anatomise the policy inefficiencies.`
- **Qualified synthesis:** `...a more comprehensive, sustainable and appropriate model in the South African context.`
- **Seminar — punch + pivot:** `...This leads us to the idea:` then next beat.
- **Seminar — honest limit:** `...Future research is required to explore comprehensively...`

### Endings you hate (written)

| Pattern | Why it clashes with your style |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| **“…which is very interesting.”** | Empty evaluation |
| **“…and that’s all I have to say.”** | Anti-climactic |
| **“In conclusion, we can see that…”** | You section-label conclusions instead |
| **“As you can see,”** | Not in your academic habit |
| **Trailing “etc.”** without list earned | You enumerate properly or use “among other indicators” |
| **Unhedged “proves”** | You use *preliminary*, *suggests*, *indicates* |

### Paragraph close pattern (written)

Last sentence of a subsection often:

1. **Ties back to RQ or aim**, or  
2. **Introduces limitation / next section**, or  
3. **Cites one authoritative source** as full stop.

## 7. Vocabulary & Phrasing You Gravitate Toward

### Signature academic phrases (use naturally)

- holistic perspective  
- allowing for notable exceptions  
- social determinants of health  
- fragmented risk pools / two-tier healthcare system  
- financial risk protection  
- pooling and resource allocation  
- post-apartheid period  
- preliminary answer  
- agrees upon / appropriate yet  
- explicit account taken of the limitations  
- plan of development  
- financing, purchasing, pooling and benefit structures  
- not on the basis of ability to pay  
- exacerbated by (corruption, shortages — as intensifier, not sole cause)  
- anatomise (policy inefficiencies — your word in practice essay)  
- whilst (investigating…) — British/formal register  
- obtainment of (services) — formal, slightly dated; you use it  

### Words you use for precision (not hype)

- inadequate / inadequacies  
- inefficiencies / impediments  
- equitable / accessibility  
- consolidated / fragmented  
- transparent accountability mechanisms  
- comparable currency / purchasing power parity  

### Seminar / spoken colour (don’t put in essays unless quoting)

- “for your viewing pleasure”  
- “hyper critical”  
- “infinity gauntlet” (metaphor for four BSC perspectives)  
- “correct in our hearts”  
- “flat earth society would like to thank…”  
- “follow the sound of my voice”  
- “take a mental note”  
- “come to grips with the research”  

### Phrasing you dislike (all registers)

- **“That matters.” / “This matters.”** as a standalone narrator beat (video/scripts) — prove significance with evidence; don’t repeatedly tell the audience to care.
- **Emphatic `did` + verb** without a contrast job: *“The economy did grow”* → prefer *“The economy grew”*. Also *did fail*, *did change*, *did see* when plain past tense carries the meaning. OK when stressing opposition: *“Politics changed; ownership did not.”*
- “very unique”, “quite literally”  
- “it goes without saying” (you say it explicitly instead)  
- “needless to say”  
- “at the end of the day” (unless ironic in seminar)  
- “game-changer” / “destroyed” (YouTube avoids hype; academic avoids it too)  
- “just” minimizing precision (“just corruption”) — you separate layers

## 8. Citations, Definitions & Numbers

### What you like

- **Define acronyms on first use:** UHC, NHI, PHC, PPE, WHO, SDGs, CHE, OOPS  
- **In-text (Author, year)** — often multiple authors chained  
- **Primary policy docs:** NDoH bills, White Papers, National Development Plan  
- **Quantify when possible:** “ten times greater”, “84%”, “6.9% mortality”, “more than 10 times” ventilator demand  
- **Name the indicator then define it** (UHC Service Coverage Index, SDG 3.8.2, etc.)  
- **Footnote-style source URLs** in references (`unpublished` web sources with access dates — 2021 style)

### What you dislike

- Claims with **no citation** in lit-heavy sections  
- **Round numbers without source** when precision is available  
- **Mixing tenses** in policy description (you mostly use present for current law, past for reforms)  
- **Vague “studies show”** — you name Marten, Coovadia, Van den Heever, etc.

## 9. How You Speak (Seminar Voice — For Oral Scripts)

This is **how you sound** when teaching or presenting — distinct from essay PDFs.

### Delivery personality

- **Structured tour guide:** You announce the framework first (Actuarial Control Cycle), then walk the room through it.  
- **Inclusive lecturer:** “we want you to be able to answer”, “we’re just curious”, “no pressure”.  
- **Self-aware:** Jokes about boring journals, coffee breaks for the supervisor, camera off for “viewing pleasure”.  
- **Critical but fair:** You steelman the paper, then list methodology flaws (sample size, thematic bias, undefined stopping rule).  
- **Actuarial brain on display:** Warren Buffett / operational vs external risk; control cycle feedback loops; “statistics graduates” framing.  

### Speech patterns

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| **Question → answer** | “But why does performance measurement play an instrumental role…? The reason behind this is because…” |
| **Imagine / Let’s** | “Imagine you find yourself at the cusp of exponential growth.” |
| **Thus pivot** | “Thus, two thirds of the agenda…” / “Thus, the basis of this paper is…” |
| **This begs the question** | Formal-ish insert in spoken register |
| **Analogy stack** | Hamurabi → Pacioli → BSC → Kelvin quote |
| **Slide sync** | `(slide 14)`, `[Next Slide]`, stage directions in brackets |
| **Poll choreography** | “Abdullah is running a quick poll now. Abdullah what are the results looking like?” |
| **Reiterate roadmap** | “Lets reiterate what has been said. There are three main approaches…” |

### Sentence starts when speaking (OK here, not in essays)

- Okay. So…  
- Well, …  
- Now, …  
- But …  
- So the question became: “…”  
- Interesting, so options A, B and C…  

### What you hate when speaking (infer from critiques)

- Reading slides verbatim without narration  
- **Unstructured paper** you had to “readjust” for the audience  
- **Subjective thematic analysis** without independent recheck (you praise when they did recheck)  
- **Stopping interviews** when interviewer *feels* done — you flag this explicitly  
- **Over-weighting one interview group** in sample — you do the math (6 vs 5 vs 5)

## 11. What You Dislike (Summary — Do Not Generate This)

**Openers**

- In this essay I will… / Today I’m going to talk about…  
- So, / Well, / Okay, (written submissions)  
- I think / I believe (without evidence)  
- Everyone knows…  

**Logic**

- Single-cause policy explanations  
- Correlation stated as proof  
- Ignoring metric or data limitations  
- Attacking literature without acknowledging strengths  

**Tone**

- Hype adjectives (amazing, insane, destroyed) in formal writing  
- Sarcasm in PDFs (save for seminar)  
- “Basically”, “literally”, “kind of”, “sort of”  

**Structure**

- Wall of text with no numbering  
- Missing references on empirical claims  
- Conclusion that introduces **new** arguments not previewed in aim  
- Bullet points with no lead-in sentence  

**Speaking**

- Monotone slide reading  
- No agenda or “by the end you will know…”  
- Polls without purpose

## 13. Example Blocks for AI Imitation

### Good — introduction (your pattern)

```text
1.1 Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is the obtainment of a comprehensive range of
health services with the certainty of financial risk protection. It can be achieved via
the establishment of accessible, equitable and quality health and financial risk
protection systems. (Evans, 2013)

1.2 Given the limited resources available, it is believed that this objective requires
the adequate and strategic pooling of both public and private resources
(South African National Department of Health, 1997). Since the introduction of
the national health insurance initiatives in the post-apartheid period, South Africa
has technically complied with the basic requirements of UHC, however various
inadequacies have existed within the policies aimed at pooling and resource
allocation.
```

### Bad — not your voice

```text
So in this essay I'm going to talk about UHC in South Africa. Everyone knows
healthcare is a mess. Basically the government failed because of corruption and
we need to fix it. In conclusion, NHI is the answer.
```

### Good — spoken handoff (seminar)

```text
Okay. So for your viewing and auditory experience, we have structured this seminar
around a familiar framework, namely the Actuarial Control cycle. Throughout this
seminar, we will systematically analyse the existing problem which the authors aimed
to address, thereafter we will introduce you to the past proposed solutions on the
topic… Finally to note, we will aim to continuously "monitor" the proposed and
resulting solutions via discussions on their limitations…
```

### Good — critique (seminar / your analytical habit)

```text
The structure of the argument and explanation of the various approaches seemed to be
rather amorphous. We had to take great care to actually readjust the explanations and
distinguish the model focuses in order to ensure that it was easy to follow.
```

## 14. Instructions for Future AI Sessions

When writing **as Abdullah — academic**:

1. Ask: **essay, research paper, or seminar script?**  
2. Use **numbered sections** matching length (short = 1.1–1.3; long = full hierarchy).  
3. Put **research question(s)** in quotes or `−` list by end of introduction.  
4. Target **~22 words mean sentence length** with ~⅓ short and ~⅙ long sentences.  
5. Open paragraphs with **The / This / Given / In aiming** — not “So,” or “I think”.  
6. Turn with **However / Unfortunately / Despite / yet** — not “But” at sentence one in PDFs.  
7. Close sections with **citation, limitation, or forward reference** — never “In conclusion,”.  
8. Always include **limitations or future work** in discussion.  
9. For SA health/econ topics: **two-tier / pooling / fragmentation** vocabulary is native — use correctly.  
10. **Never** paste seminar jokes into essays.
